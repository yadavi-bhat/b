Topic 01 · Nutrition

Carbohydrates

The body's primary fuel source — made of Carbon, Hydrogen & Oxygen.

What are they?

  • Nutrients made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • The main source of energy for the body.
  • Brain cells and red blood cells depend ONLY on carbohydrates for energy.

Food Sources

  • 🌾 Grains – rice, wheat, bread
  • 🥛 Dairy – milk, yogurt
  • 🍌 Fruits – bananas, apples
  • 🫘 Legumes – beans, lentils
  • 🥔 Starchy vegetables – potatoes, corn
  • 🥕 Non-starchy vegetables – carrots, spinach (small amounts)

Two Main Types

  • Simple carbohydrates – simple sugars (fruits, sweets) → fast energy
  • Complex carbohydrates – fiber, starch, glycogen (whole grains, legumes) → slow sustained energy

Topic 02 · Nutrition

Lipids

Fats & oils — energy storage, insulation, and cell membrane builders.

What are they?

  • Made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — like carbohydrates.
  • Key difference: lipids are water-insoluble (don't mix with water).

Sources

  • 🧈 Butter · 🫒 Oils · 🥩 Meat
  • 🥛 Dairy products · 🥜 Nuts & seeds
  • Many processed foods

Types of Lipids

  • Triglycerides – main form of fat in food and body
  • Phospholipids – important for cell membranes
  • Sterols – e.g. cholesterol

Functions

  • Store and provide energy
  • Build cell membranes
  • Protect organs (fat padding)
  • Keep body warm (insulation)
  • Help in body regulation functions (hormones)

Topic 03 · Nutrition

Proteins

The body's building blocks — chains of amino acids doing everything.

Composition

  • Large molecules made of chains of amino acids.
  • Amino acids = simple subunits made of carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, and nitrogen.
  • Nitrogen is what makes proteins unique compared to carbs/lipids.

Food Sources

  • 🥩 Meats · 🧀 Dairy products · 🐟 Seafood
  • 🫘 Beans · 🥜 Nuts · 🌱 Seeds (plant-based)

Functions

  • Provide structure to bones, muscles, and skin
  • Conduct most chemical reactions in the body (enzymes are proteins!)
  • Important for growth, repair, and immune function

⭐ Exam Tip

Topic 04 · Nutrition

Water

The universal solvent — 60%+ of your body is water.

Composition

  • Does NOT contain carbon — unique among nutrients.
  • Made of 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen (H₂O) per molecule.
  • More than 60% of total body weight is water.

Why is Water Essential?

  • Without it, nothing could be transported in or out of the body.
  • Chemical reactions would not occur.
  • Organs would not be cushioned.
  • Body temperature would fluctuate widely.

Daily Intake

  • An adult consumes just over 2 litres per day from food and drink combined.

Topic 06 · Digestion

The Digestive System

Food → Energy: a 7-organ journey from mouth to anus.

What is it?

  • Group of organs that convert food into energy.
  • Consists of the GI (gastrointestinal) tract + accessory organs (liver, pancreas, gallbladder).
  • GI tract organs (in order): Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine → Rectum → Anus

Parts Explained

  • Mouth – Teeth break food; saliva softens it; tongue pushes to pharynx.
  • Pharynx – Connects mouth to oesophagus; also used for breathing.
  • Oesophagus – Long muscular tube; moves food to stomach via peristalsis.
  • Stomach – Muscular bag (left upper belly, below diaphragm); stores food; makes digestive juices + HCl.
  • Mucous – Watery substance; protects stomach wall from its own acid.
  • Digestive Enzymes – Break large molecules into smaller absorbable forms.
  • HCl (Hydrochloric Acid) – Kills bacteria; helps break down food.
  • Small Intestine – Most digestion & nutrient absorption happens here.
  • Large Intestine – Absorbs water & electrolytes; forms solid waste (stool).
  • Rectum – Stores waste until expulsion.

Topic 07 · Digestion

Accessory Digestive Organs

Pancreas · Liver · Gallbladder — the support team of digestion.

Pancreas

  • Large gland located just behind the stomach.
  • Makes and releases digestive enzymes.
  • Enzymes help break down food during digestion (sent to small intestine).
  • Key enzymes: amylase (carbs), lipase (fats), trypsin/protease (proteins).

Liver

  • Found on the right side of the abdomen.
  • Produces bile — a fluid that helps digest fats in the small intestine.
  • Also detoxifies blood, produces blood proteins, stores glycogen.

Gallbladder

  • Small organ located under the liver.
  • Stores and recycles bile made by the liver.
  • Bile is released into the small intestine when food arrives there.

"PiLGrim" → Pancreas (enzymes) · Liver (bile maker) · Gallbladder (bile store). All deliver to the small intestine!

⭐ Exam Tip

Liver = MAKES bile. Gallbladder = STORES bile. Pancreas = makes ENZYMES. Bile breaks down FATS (lipids).

Topic 08 · Respiration

The Respiratory System

Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli — the O₂/CO₂ highway.

Overview

  • Made up of organs/tissues that help us breathe.
  • Takes in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide.
  • Oxygen → carried by blood → used by cells → cellular respiration → produces CO₂ as waste.

Key Parts

  • Trachea (Windpipe) – Connects larynx to lungs; C-shaped cartilage rings keep it open.
  • Bronchi – Trachea splits into 2 bronchi (one per lung).
  • Bronchioles – Bronchi split into smaller tubes → bronchioles.
  • Alveoli – Tiny, single-celled sacs at the end of bronchioles; thin walls allow gas exchange.
  • Lungs – Main breathing organs; located either side of heart in chest cavity.

Gas Exchange in Lungs

  • Happens by diffusion — molecules move from high → low pressure.
  • O₂ in alveoli = higher pressure → moves INTO blood.
  • CO₂ in blood = higher pressure → moves INTO alveoli → exhaled.

🧠 Mnemonic

"Tracy Bought Bronchioles and Alveoli Lunches" → Trachea · Bronchi · Bronchioles · Alveoli · Lungs. Alveoli = tiny sacs = gas exchange!

Topic 09 · Respiration

Aerobic vs Anaerobic Respiration

"With air" vs "without air" — same glucose, different products.

Aerobic Respiration

  • "With air" — uses oxygen to make energy from food.
  • Oxygen-rich air → travels to cells → glucose broken down using O₂.
  • Products: Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy (ATP).
  • Helps plants, animals, humans to grow and move.
Aerobic: Glucose + Oxygen → CO₂ + Water + Energy

Anaerobic Respiration

  • "Without air" — no oxygen needed.
  • Occurs in lower plants and microorganisms (e.g. yeast).
  • Glucose broken down into alcohol + carbon dioxide + energy.
  • Less energy released than aerobic.
Anaerobic: Glucose → Alcohol + CO₂ + Energy

🧠 Mnemonic

Aerobic = "AerOBIC = O₂ IN BODY." Anaerobic = "AN = withAN oxygen." Products: Aerobic → CO₂+H₂O+LOTS of energy. Anaerobic → Alcohol+CO₂+LITTLE energy.

Topic 10 · Excretion

The Excretory System

Kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra — the body's waste management.

What is Excretion?

  • Process of getting rid of metabolic wastes from the body.
  • After nutrients are used in metabolic reactions, non-useful & toxic substances must be removed.
  • Aquatic animals → excrete ammonia.
  • Birds & insects → excrete uric acid.
  • Humans → excrete urea and urine.

The 4 Main Organs

  • Kidneys (pair) – Filter blood; remove waste by making urine; maintain electrolyte & hormone balance.
  • Ureters (pair) – Tubes carrying urine FROM kidneys TO bladder.
  • Urinary Bladder – Temporarily stores urine.
  • Urethra – Tube carrying urine from bladder to outside.

Inside the Kidney — Nephron Parts

  • Loop of Henle – U-shaped tube; reabsorbs water & salts from filtrate.
  • Distal Convoluted Tubule – Adjusts salts & substances to keep blood balanced.
  • Collecting Duct – Carries filtered liquid to ureter; final urine formed here.

Kidney = filters blood = makes urine. Loop of Henle = reabsorbs water & salts. Ureter ≠ Urethra (ureters = 2 tubes from kidneys; urethra = 1 tube from bladder to outside).

Topic 11 · Artificial Organs

Heart, Artificial Heart & Pacemaker

The pump that never stops — and the devices that keep it going.

How the Heart Works

  • Deoxygenated blood from body → enters Right Atrium → Right Ventricle → pumped to lungs (to pick up O₂).
  • Oxygenated blood → returns to Left Atrium → Left Ventricle → pumped to rest of body.
  • Heartbeat controlled by electrical signals → steady rhythm.
  • ECG (Electrocardiogram) – records electrical signals; wave-like graph showing heart function.

Artificial Heart (Total Artificial Heart)

  • Replaces damaged ventricles and valves.
  • Ventricles made of polyurethane (strong, flexible medical material).
  • A driver machine (outside the body) controls the pump.
  • Usually a "bridge to transplant" — temporary until a donor heart is available.

Pacemaker

  • Small device placed under the skin in the chest.
  • Treats bradycardia (slow heartbeat) and arrhythmias (irregular heartbeat).
  • Sends electrical signals to heart to maintain proper beat.
  • Generator – battery + electronics that create electrical signals.
  • Leads – thin wires connecting generator to heart.
  • Electrodes – at tips of leads; deliver signals to heart muscle.

Topic 12 · Artificial Organs

Artificial Lungs & Kidneys

ECMO for lungs · Hemodialysis & peritoneal for kidneys.

Artificial Lungs

  • Help people with ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) or ALI (Acute Lung Injury).
  • Support lungs while they heal.

Two Types of Artificial Lungs

  • Membrane Oxygenators – special membrane moves O₂ and CO₂ between blood and air; blood flows through membrane.
  • Extracorporeal Lung Assist Devices – remove CO₂ from blood and add O₂; give lungs rest to heal.
  • Best example: ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation) – for severe breathing problems.

Artificial Kidney

  • Filters waste, excess fluids, and toxins when natural kidneys fail.
  • Used for kidney failure / ESRD (End-Stage Renal Disease).

Types of Artificial Kidneys

  • Hemodialysis – blood drawn out → cleaned through dialyzer filter → returned. Done 3–4×/week in clinic.
  • Peritoneal Dialysis – sterile fluid into abdomen; peritoneum = natural filter; fluid drained & replaced. Done at home.

  • Storage: Acts as a temporary storage space for food, allowing the body time to digest it.
  • Mechanical digestion: Stomach muscles churn and mix the food with gastric juices.

  • Propulsion: Peristalsis moves food through oesophagus into stomach.
  • Chemical breakdown: Stomach's HCl + enzymes break food into smaller molecules; small intestine receives bile and pancreatic enzymes to fully break down carbs, fats, and proteins.
  • Absorption: Small intestine's large surface area (villi and microvilli) absorbs nutrients — glucose, amino acids, fatty acids — into the blood.
  • Water absorption: Large intestine absorbs remaining water and electrolytes.
  • Elimination: Indigestible waste formed into stool; stored in rectum; expelled via anus.
  • The digestive system is a group of organs that convert food into energy and nutrients the body can use.
  • It consists of the GI tract (Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine → Rectum → Anus) and accessory organs (Liver, Pancreas, Gallbladder).
  • Food undergoes mechanical (physical breaking) and chemical (enzyme/acid) digestion.
  • Nutrients are absorbed mainly in the small intestine.
  • Water is absorbed in the large intestine.
  • Waste is expelled through the rectum and anus.

  • Lungs: Main organs of respiration. Located on either side of the heart in the chest cavity. Primary function is gas exchange between air and blood.
  • Diaphragm: Dome-shaped muscle below lungs. Contracts (flattens) to increase chest volume for inhalation; relaxes (rises) for exhalation.
  • Nasal cavity/Nose: Filters, warms, and moistens air before it enters the lungs.
  • Trachea: Passageway for air from larynx to lungs; C-shaped cartilage rings maintain openness.

  • 3. Lungs: The primary organs of respiration, located on both sides of the heart. Their main job is to facilitate gas exchange — oxygen from inhaled air enters the bloodstream, while CO₂ from the blood is exhaled.
  • The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscular structure located below the lungs, separating the chest cavity from the abdominal cavity.
  • Inhalation (Breathing In): Diaphragm contracts and flattens downward → chest cavity volume increases → air pressure inside decreases → air rushes into lungs (from high pressure outside to low pressure inside).
  • Exhalation (Breathing Out): Diaphragm relaxes and moves upward (dome shape restored) → chest cavity volume decreases → air pressure increases → air is pushed out of lungs.
  • The diaphragm works alongside the intercostal muscles (between ribs) to control breathing.
  • It is the primary muscle responsible for the mechanical movement of breathing.

🍎 Nutrition — Macronutrients & Micronutrients
1.Which nutrient is the PRIMARY source of energy for the body?
Carbohydrates are the body's main and preferred energy source. Simple sugars provide quick energy; complex carbs provide sustained energy. Brain cells and RBCs ONLY use carbohydrates for energy.
2.Which cells in the body depend ONLY on carbohydrates for their energy?
Brain cells (neurons) and red blood cells (RBCs) are entirely dependent on glucose (a carbohydrate) for their energy needs. They cannot use fats or proteins as energy sources.
3.Fiber, starch, and glycogen are examples of which type of carbohydrate?
Complex carbohydrates include fiber, starch, and glycogen. They are found in whole grains, legumes, and starchy vegetables. Simple carbohydrates are the sugars found in fruits and sweets — they give quick energy.
4.Which property makes lipids UNIQUE compared to carbohydrates?
Lipids and carbohydrates are both made of C, H, O. The key difference is that lipids are WATER-INSOLUBLE (they don't mix with water), while carbohydrates dissolve in water. This property is what defines lipids.
5.Which type of lipid is the MAIN form of fat found in both food and the human body?
Triglycerides are the most abundant form of fat in both food and the human body. They consist of a glycerol backbone with 3 fatty acid chains. Phospholipids form cell membranes; sterols (like cholesterol) serve hormonal and structural roles.
6.Which element present in proteins makes them chemically DISTINCT from carbohydrates and lipids?
Proteins contain C, O, H, and NITROGEN. Nitrogen is the element that distinguishes proteins from carbohydrates and lipids, which only contain C, H, and O. This nitrogen is found in the amino group (–NH₂) of each amino acid.
7.Proteins are composed of chains of:
Proteins are large molecules composed of chains of amino acids. Each amino acid is a small subunit made of C, O, H, and N. The sequence and folding of these chains determines the protein's structure and function (enzymes, structural proteins, antibodies, etc.).
8.What percentage of total body weight is water?
More than 60% of total body weight is water. This highlights how critical water is — it acts as a solvent for all metabolic reactions, a transport medium, a cushion for organs, and a temperature regulator. Adults consume ~2 litres daily.
9.Which mineral is responsible for oxygen transport in the blood?
Iron is a trace mineral that carries oxygen in the blood — it is a key component of haemoglobin in red blood cells. Iron also assists in energy production. Iron deficiency leads to anaemia (lack of oxygen transport).
10.Which mineral is essential for THYROID HORMONE production?
Iodine is a trace mineral essential for thyroid hormone production (thyroxine). Thyroid hormones regulate metabolism and growth. Iodine deficiency leads to goitre (enlarged thyroid gland) and hypothyroidism.
11.Which mineral helps in tooth decay prevention and bone health maintenance?
Fluoride is a trace mineral known for maintaining bone and teeth health and preventing tooth decay. It strengthens tooth enamel making it resistant to acid attack by bacteria. That's why fluoride is added to drinking water and toothpaste.
12.Calcium performs which of the following functions? (Select the MOST complete answer)
Calcium is the most multi-functional major mineral: (1) Bone and teeth health maintenance, (2) Nerve transmission, (3) Muscle contraction, (4) Blood clotting. This is a favourite exam question — calcium does ALL four!
🦷 Digestive System
13.Which enzyme in saliva begins the chemical digestion of starch in the mouth?
Salivary amylase (ptyalin) is the enzyme in saliva that begins breaking down starch (a complex carbohydrate) into simpler sugars, starting digestion in the mouth. Pepsin works in the stomach on proteins; lipase on fats; trypsin in the small intestine on proteins.
14.Which enzyme found in the stomach primarily digests PROTEINS?
Pepsin is the primary protein-digesting enzyme in the stomach. It works optimally in the acidic environment created by HCl (hydrochloric acid) in the stomach. Pepsin breaks large protein molecules into smaller peptide chains.
15.Which enzyme is produced by the pancreas to digest FATS (lipids)?
Pancreatic lipase is produced by the pancreas and is released into the small intestine to digest fats (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. It works together with bile (from the liver/gallbladder) which emulsifies fats first.
16.Trypsin is a digestive enzyme that primarily digests:
Trypsin is a proteolytic (protein-digesting) enzyme produced by the pancreas and released into the small intestine. It breaks down proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptide chains and amino acids for absorption.
17.The LIVER's main digestive function is to:
The liver's main digestive role is to PRODUCE bile — a fluid that emulsifies (breaks up) fats in the small intestine, making them easier for lipase to digest. Note: the gallbladder STORES bile; the liver MAKES it.
18.The GALLBLADDER's function is to:
The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver. When fatty food enters the small intestine, the gallbladder contracts and releases bile into the small intestine to aid fat digestion. Remember: Liver = MAKES bile; Gallbladder = STORES bile.
19.Where does MOST nutrient absorption occur in the digestive system?
Most digestion and nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. It has a large surface area (due to villi and microvilli) which maximises absorption of glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals into the bloodstream.
20.What is CHYME?
Chyme is the semi-liquid, acidic mixture formed when stomach muscles churn food together with gastric juices (HCl + enzymes + mucous). It is gradually released from the stomach into the small intestine for further digestion.
21.What does hydrochloric acid (HCl) in the stomach do?
HCl (hydrochloric acid) in the stomach creates a highly acidic environment (pH ~1–2) that: (1) kills harmful bacteria/germs in food, (2) denatures proteins making them easier for pepsin to digest, (3) activates pepsinogen into pepsin.
22.The PANCREAS sends digestive enzymes to which organ?
The pancreas secretes digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, trypsin/protease) into the small intestine via the pancreatic duct. These enzymes work in the alkaline environment of the small intestine to break down carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.
🫁 Respiratory System
23.What keeps the TRACHEA open and prevents it from collapsing?
The walls of the trachea contain C-shaped cartilaginous (cartilage) rings that keep the trachea open and rigid, preventing it from collapsing. The "C" shape allows the oesophagus (which runs behind the trachea) to expand when food is swallowed.
24.The trachea divides into two structures called:
The trachea divides into two bronchi — one for each lung (left bronchus and right bronchus). Each bronchus then divides into smaller bronchioles, which further branch until they end in alveoli. This branching pattern is called the bronchial tree.
25.Where does gas exchange (O₂ and CO₂) take place in the lungs?
Alveoli are tiny, single-celled air sacs at the ends of bronchioles. Their extremely thin walls and high surface area enable gas exchange — O₂ diffuses from alveoli into the blood, and CO₂ diffuses from blood into alveoli to be exhaled. Alveoli are the functional units of the lung.
26.Gas exchange in the alveoli occurs through which process?
Gas exchange occurs through DIFFUSION — the movement of molecules from a region of HIGH concentration/pressure to LOW. O₂ is at higher pressure in alveoli than in blood (so it moves in). CO₂ is at higher pressure in blood than in alveoli (so it moves out).
27.When oxygen pressure in alveoli is HIGH compared to blood, oxygen moves:
During diffusion, gas moves from high pressure to low pressure. Since O₂ in alveoli has HIGHER pressure than O₂ in blood, it diffuses from alveoli INTO blood. Similarly, CO₂ in blood has HIGHER pressure than in alveoli, so it moves INTO alveoli.
28.The diaphragm's role in breathing is:
The diaphragm is the main muscle of breathing. It contracts (flattens) to expand the chest cavity → air flows in (inhalation). It relaxes (rises) to decrease chest volume → air is pushed out (exhalation). It does NOT do gas exchange — alveoli do that.
29.The process of cells using oxygen to break down glucose for energy is called:
Cellular respiration is the process by which cells use oxygen to break down glucose (food) to produce ATP (energy), CO₂, and water. This is aerobic respiration at the cellular level. The respiratory system delivers O₂ and removes CO₂ to support this process.
💨 Aerobic vs Anaerobic Respiration
30.The equation for AEROBIC respiration is:
Aerobic respiration equation: Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + Energy. It uses oxygen and releases MORE energy than anaerobic. The energy is used for all body functions including movement, growth, and metabolism.
31.The equation for ANAEROBIC respiration is:
Anaerobic respiration equation: Glucose → Alcohol + Carbon Dioxide + Energy. No oxygen is needed. It occurs in lower plants, microorganisms (yeast), and during extreme exercise in muscles. It produces less energy than aerobic respiration.
32.Which type of respiration produces MORE energy?
Aerobic respiration produces significantly MORE energy (up to 36–38 ATP per glucose molecule) compared to anaerobic respiration (only 2 ATP per glucose). This is because aerobic respiration fully oxidises glucose using oxygen, releasing more energy.
33.Anaerobic respiration typically occurs in which organisms?
According to the notes, anaerobic respiration usually happens in lower plants and microorganisms (like yeast). Yeast fermentation (producing alcohol and CO₂ from glucose without oxygen) is a classic example used in bread-making and brewing.
🫘 Excretory System & Kidney
34.Excretion is defined as:
Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste products — substances that are produced by the body's own chemical reactions and are no longer useful or could be harmful if retained (e.g. urea, CO₂, water, uric acid).
35.Humans mainly excrete waste in the form of:
Humans excrete urea (formed from protein breakdown in the liver) and urine. Compare: aquatic animals excrete ammonia (very toxic but diluted in water); birds and insects excrete uric acid (solid, water-conserving). Urea is less toxic than ammonia and can be concentrated in urine.
36.Which organ is described as a "complex filtration system" in the excretory system?
The kidney is described as a complex filtration system that: separates useful substances by reabsorption, removes toxic substances by making urine, and maintains electrolyte/hormone balance. The basic filtering unit of the kidney is the nephron.
37.The URETERS carry urine FROM ___ TO ___:
There are two ureters (one from each kidney). They carry urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder. The urine then accumulates in the bladder until excreted through the urethra. Remember: Ureter ≠ Urethra (urethra = single tube from bladder to outside).
38.The Loop of Henle in the nephron is responsible for:
The Loop of Henle is a U-shaped tube in the nephron. Its main function is to reabsorb water and salts from the filtrate back into the body, concentrating the urine and ensuring the body doesn't lose too much water or essential electrolytes.
39.Where is final urine formed in the nephron?
The Collecting Duct is where final urine is formed. It receives filtered liquid from multiple nephrons, makes final adjustments to water and ion content, and carries the concentrated urine toward the ureter. This is the last stage before urine leaves the kidney.
💉 Dialysis
40.In HEMODIALYSIS, which device is used to filter the blood?
In hemodialysis, blood is taken out of the body and passed through a dialyzer (also called an artificial kidney machine) which contains a special filter that removes waste, toxins, and extra fluid. The cleaned blood is then returned to the body.
41.In PERITONEAL dialysis, the natural filter used is:
In peritoneal dialysis, a sterile fluid is introduced into the abdominal cavity. The PERITONEUM (the membrane lining the abdomen) acts as a natural semi-permeable filter — waste and excess fluid from blood pass into the dialysate fluid, which is then drained and replaced.
42.Peritoneal dialysis is advantageous over hemodialysis because:
Peritoneal dialysis can be done at home by the patient themselves, offering much more flexibility than hemodialysis (which requires a clinic visit 3–4 times per week). This makes it more convenient and improves quality of life for some patients.
🤖 Artificial Organs & Medical Devices
43.The Total Artificial Heart's ventricles are made of:
The Total Artificial Heart has ventricles made of POLYURETHANE — a strong, flexible, biocompatible polymer used in medical devices. It mimics the flexibility and strength of natural heart tissue, allowing it to pump blood effectively.
44.The Total Artificial Heart is often called a "bridge to transplant" because:
The Total Artificial Heart is a TEMPORARY solution — a "bridge to transplant." It maintains blood circulation while the patient waits for a suitable donor heart for transplantation. After transplant, the artificial heart is removed.
45.What device records the electrical signals of the heart as a wave-like graph?
An ECG (Electrocardiogram) records the electrical signals of the heart and displays them as a wave-like graph. It helps doctors see if the heart is beating regularly, identify arrhythmias, detect heart attacks, and assess overall heart health.
46.A pacemaker is used to treat which heart conditions?
A pacemaker treats BRADYCARDIA (abnormally slow heartbeat) and ARRHYTHMIAS (irregular heartbeats). It sends electrical signals to the heart to keep it beating at a regular, appropriate rate. It does NOT treat heart attacks directly.
47.The thin wires in a pacemaker that carry electrical signals to the heart are called:
Leads are the thin insulated wires in a pacemaker. They connect the generator (battery/electronics) to the heart. At the tips of the leads are ELECTRODES, which physically deliver electrical impulses to the heart muscle to stimulate a beat.
48.ECMO stands for:
ECMO = Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation. "Extracorporeal" means outside the body. It uses a membrane to oxygenate blood and remove CO₂ outside the body, then returns the treated blood. It is used for severe ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) or ALI (Acute Lung Injury).
49.Artificial lungs are used to treat patients with:
Artificial lungs (including ECMO) are used for patients with ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) or ALI (Acute Lung Injury) — severe conditions where the lungs cannot provide enough oxygen or remove CO₂. The artificial lung takes over while the patient's lungs rest and heal.
50.Artificial kidneys (dialysis) are primarily used for patients with:
Artificial kidneys (both hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis) are used for patients with kidney failure or End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) — where the kidneys can no longer effectively filter the blood. Dialysis takes over the kidney's function of removing waste and excess fluid.
51.Which part of the heart first receives deoxygenated (impure) blood from the body?
Deoxygenated blood from the body first enters the RIGHT ATRIUM. From there it goes to the right ventricle → pumped to lungs to pick up oxygen → returns to left atrium → left ventricle → pumped to the whole body. This is the double circulation pathway.
52.Membrane Oxygenators used as artificial lungs work by:
Membrane Oxygenators use a semi-permeable membrane. Blood flows on one side; oxygen-rich air on the other. O₂ diffuses from air side into blood; CO₂ diffuses from blood into air side — exactly mimicking what alveoli do in the natural lung.
