Topic 01 · Nutrition

Carbohydrates

The body's primary fuel source — made of Carbon, Hydrogen & Oxygen.

What are they?

  • Nutrients made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • The main source of energy for the body.
  • Brain cells and red blood cells depend ONLY on carbohydrates for energy.

Food Sources

  • 🌾 Grains – rice, wheat, bread
  • 🥛 Dairy – milk, yogurt
  • 🍌 Fruits – bananas, apples
  • 🫘 Legumes – beans, lentils
  • 🥔 Starchy vegetables – potatoes, corn
  • 🥕 Non-starchy vegetables – carrots, spinach (small amounts)

Two Main Types

  • Simple carbohydrates – simple sugars (fruits, sweets) → fast energy
  • Complex carbohydrates – fiber, starch, glycogen (whole grains, legumes) → slow sustained energy

🧠 Mnemonic

"CHO = Carbon Hydrogen Oxygen" — same letters as the formula! Remember: Grains, Dairy, Fruits, Legumes, Veggies = "Good Diet Feeds Living Vitality"

⭐ Important for Exam

Brain cells & RBCs = ONLY carbohydrate users. Simple = fast/sugar. Complex = fiber+starch+glycogen.

Topic 02 · Nutrition

Lipids

Fats & oils — energy storage, insulation, and cell membrane builders.

What are they?

  • Made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — like carbohydrates.
  • Key difference: lipids are water-insoluble (don't mix with water).

Sources

  • 🧈 Butter · 🫒 Oils · 🥩 Meat
  • 🥛 Dairy products · 🥜 Nuts & seeds
  • Many processed foods

Types of Lipids

  • Triglycerides – main form of fat in food and body
  • Phospholipids – important for cell membranes
  • Sterols – e.g. cholesterol

Functions

  • Store and provide energy
  • Build cell membranes
  • Protect organs (fat padding)
  • Keep body warm (insulation)
  • Help in body regulation functions (hormones)

Topic 03 · Nutrition

Proteins

The body's building blocks — chains of amino acids doing everything.

Composition

  • Large molecules made of chains of amino acids.
  • Amino acids = simple subunits made of carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, and nitrogen.
  • Nitrogen is what makes proteins unique compared to carbs/lipids.

Food Sources

  • 🥩 Meats · 🧀 Dairy products · 🐟 Seafood
  • 🫘 Beans · 🥜 Nuts · 🌱 Seeds (plant-based)

Functions

  • Provide structure to bones, muscles, and skin
  • Conduct most chemical reactions in the body (enzymes are proteins!)
  • Important for growth, repair, and immune function

🧠 Mnemonic

⭐ Exam Tip

Proteins = amino acids = C+O+H+N. The N is the key distinguisher. Enzymes = proteins. Sources = meat, dairy, seafood, beans, nuts, seeds.

Topic 04 · Nutrition

Water

The universal solvent — 60%+ of your body is water.

Composition

  • Does NOT contain carbon — unique among nutrients.
  • Made of 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen (H₂O) per molecule.
  • More than 60% of total body weight is water.

Why is Water Essential?

  • Without it, nothing could be transported in or out of the body.
  • Chemical reactions would not occur.
  • Organs would not be cushioned.
  • Body temperature would fluctuate widely.

Daily Intake

  • An adult consumes just over 2 litres per day from food and drink combined.

"TCOT = Transport · Chemical reactions · Organ cushioning · Temperature regulation" — water does all 4! Water = the body's logistics system.

Topic 06 · Digestion

The Digestive System

Food → Energy: a 7-organ journey from mouth to anus.

What is it?

  • Group of organs that convert food into energy.
  • Consists of the GI (gastrointestinal) tract + accessory organs (liver, pancreas, gallbladder).
  • GI tract organs (in order): Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine → Rectum → Anus

Parts Explained

  • Mouth – Teeth break food; saliva softens it; tongue pushes to pharynx.
  • Pharynx – Connects mouth to oesophagus; also used for breathing.
  • Oesophagus – Long muscular tube; moves food to stomach via peristalsis.
  • Stomach – Muscular bag (left upper belly, below diaphragm); stores food; makes digestive juices + HCl.
  • Mucous – Watery substance; protects stomach wall from its own acid.
  • Digestive Enzymes – Break large molecules into smaller absorbable forms.
  • HCl (Hydrochloric Acid) – Kills bacteria; helps break down food.
  • Small Intestine – Most digestion & nutrient absorption happens here.
  • Large Intestine – Absorbs water & electrolytes; forms solid waste (stool).
  • Rectum – Stores waste until expulsion.

Topic 07 · Digestion

Accessory Digestive Organs

Pancreas · Liver · Gallbladder — the support team of digestion.

Pancreas

  • Large gland located just behind the stomach.
  • Makes and releases digestive enzymes.
  • Enzymes help break down food during digestion (sent to small intestine).
  • Key enzymes: amylase (carbs), lipase (fats), trypsin/protease (proteins).

Liver

  • Found on the right side of the abdomen.
  • Produces bile — a fluid that helps digest fats in the small intestine.
  • Also detoxifies blood, produces blood proteins, stores glycogen.

Gallbladder

  • Small organ located under the liver.
  • Stores and recycles bile made by the liver.
  • Bile is released into the small intestine when food arrives there.

🧠 Mnemonic

"PiLGrim" → Pancreas (enzymes) · Liver (bile maker) · Gallbladder (bile store). All deliver to the small intestine!

⭐ Exam Tip

Liver = MAKES bile. Gallbladder = STORES bile. Pancreas = makes ENZYMES. Bile breaks down FATS (lipids).

Topic 08 · Respiration

The Respiratory System

Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli — the O₂/CO₂ highway.

Overview

  • Made up of organs/tissues that help us breathe.
  • Takes in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide.
  • Oxygen → carried by blood → used by cells → cellular respiration → produces CO₂ as waste.

Key Parts

  • Trachea (Windpipe) – Connects larynx to bronchi; reinforced by cartilaginous rings.
  • Bronchi & Bronchioles – Air tubes branching into left & right lungs, getting progressively smaller.
  • Alveoli – Tiny, balloon-like air sacs at the end of bronchioles where gas exchange occurs. Surrounded by rich capillary networks.
  • Diaphragm – Dome-shaped muscle below lungs that contracts to draw air in and relaxes to expel air.

Fig 3.1 — Diffusion across the respiratory membrane

💡 Physiology Fact

Diffusion is passive, driven by partial pressure gradients. Oxygen diffuses from high pressure in alveoli to low pressure in deoxygenated blood; Carbon Dioxide does the opposite.

Topic 09 · Respiration

Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Respiration

How cells generate ATP — with or without Oxygen.

Comparison Summary

FeatureAerobic RespirationAnaerobic Respiration
Oxygen RequirementYesNo
Energy YieldHigh (36-38 ATP per glucose)Low (2 ATP per glucose)
LocationCytoplasm & MitochondriaCytoplasm only
End ProductsCO₂ + H₂OLactic Acid (animals) or Ethanol + CO₂ (yeast)
EfficiencyHigh (approx. 40%)Low (approx. 2%)

Chemical Pathways

Aerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + 36-38 ATP
Anaerobic (Lactic Acid): C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 Lactic Acid + 2 ATP

🧠 Mnemonic

"Aerobic is Air-obic" — requires air (oxygen), happens in mitochondria. "Anaerobic is Anti-air" — operates without oxygen, fast but low yield (makes muscles burn with lactic acid!).

Topic 10 · Excretion

The Excretory System

Filtration, Reabsorption & Secretion — the body's water purification plant.

What is it?

  • System responsible for **eliminating metabolic waste** (urea, excess salts, water) and maintaining osmotic balance.
  • Key Organs: **Kidneys (2) → Ureters (2) → Urinary Bladder (1) → Urethra (1)**.

The Nephron (Functional Unit)

  • Each kidney contains **~1 million nephrons**.
  • **Bowman's Capsule & Glomerulus**: Blood pressure forces water and small solutes out of capillaries (Ultrafiltration).
  • **Renal Tubules**: Reabsorb essential nutrients (glucose, water, ions) back into blood; secrete waste ions into urine.

Fig 4.1 — Three steps of urine formation in nephron

🧠 Mnemonic

"F-R-S: Urine is Made of Three" → Filtration (Bowman's) · Reabsorption (Tubules to blood) · Secretion (Blood to tubules). Nephron = "Filtering Recycled Streams".

Topic 11 · Artificial Organs

Cardiac Pacemaker

Engineering the heart's rhythm — SA node replacement.

The SA Node (Natural Pacemaker)

  • Specialized muscle cells in the **right atrium** that generate electrical pulses.
  • Spreads electrical signals through the heart, causing ventricles to contract and pump blood.

Artificial Pacemaker

  • An electronic device implanted under the skin to **regulate slow or irregular heartbeats** (bradycardia).
  • Components: **Pulse Generator** (lithium battery + circuits) and **Leads/Electrodes** (threaded through veins into the heart tissue).
  • Working: Circuits sense heart's rhythm; if it drops below a threshold, the generator fires micro-pulses to stimulate contraction.

Fig 5.1 — Pacemaker system implanted in heart

⚙️ Engineering Specs

Pacemakers use **Lithium-iodine batteries** which last 7-10 years. Lead wires must withstand **millions of flexes** per year without fatigue failure.

Topic 12 · Artificial Organs

Artificial Lungs & Kidneys

Mass transfer membrane systems mimicking homeostatic filters.

Artificial Lung (Oxygenator)

  • Used during cardiopulmonary bypass (heart-lung machine) or ECMO.
  • Consists of thousands of **hollow fiber membranes**.
  • Blood flows around fibers; sweep gas (pure oxygen) flows inside fibers.
  • **Passive diffusion** forces oxygen into blood and CO₂ out.

Artificial Kidney (Hemodialyzer)

  • Filters urea and excess waste from blood during kidney failure (renal dialysis).
  • Uses **semi-permeable hollow fibers** wrapped in a dialyzer casing.
  • Blood flows through fibers, while a salt solution (dialysate) flows around them in a **countercurrent direction**.
  • Urea and excess salts diffuse down their concentration gradient into the dialysate, purifying the blood.

Fig 5.2 — Countercurrent mass exchange in hemodialysis

💡 Dialysis Physics

Countercurrent flow maintains a **constant concentration gradient** along the entire length of the filter, maximizing filtration efficiency compared to co-current flow.

⚡ Quick Recap & Mnemonics

Quick reference summaries and memory joggers for human physiology and bio-device engineering.

Nutrition

Macronutrients vs Micronutrients

  • Macronutrients: Carbs, Lipids, Proteins, Water. Needed in grams. Provide energy and structural blocks.
  • Micronutrients: Vitamins, Minerals (Major & Trace). Needed in micro/milligrams. Act as regulators and coenzymes.
Digestion

The GI Tract Highway

  • Mouth: Salivary Amylase breaks starch.
  • Stomach: HCl + Pepsin digests proteins.
  • Small Intestine: Villi absorb 90% nutrients.
  • Large Intestine: Recovers water and salts.
Respiration

Aerobic vs Anaerobic Yield

  • Aerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 36 ATP. Occurs in mitochondria. End products CO₂ and H₂O.
  • Anaerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 ATP + Lactic Acid. Occurs in cytoplasm. High speed but low energy efficiency.
Excretion

Urine Formation in Nephron

  • Glomerular Filtration: Passive ultrafiltration of fluid based on hydrostatic pressure.
  • Tubular Reabsorption: Transporting useful nutrients (glucose, water) back to blood.
  • Tubular Secretion: Actively discarding wastes (K+, H+) into tubules.
Artificial Organs

Engineering Principles

  • Biocompatibility: Avoiding blood clotting (thrombosis) or immune rejection.
  • Countercurrent Flow: Used in dialysis to maintain mass transfer gradients.
  • Pacemaker Battery: Lithium-iodine battery ensures long life span (7-10 years).
Mnemonics

Memory Joggers

  • "F-R-S": Filtration, Reabsorption, Secretion (nephron steps).

The six major classes of nutrients required by the body are divided into macronutrients and micronutrients:

  • Carbohydrates: The body's primary energy source (e.g., starch, sugars).
  • Lipids (Fats): Used for long-term energy storage, organ cushioning, cell membranes, and hormones.
  • Proteins: The building blocks of tissues and enzymes that catalyze metabolic reactions.
  • Water: The universal solvent, transporting substances, regulating temperature, and cushioning joints.
  • Vitamins: Organic molecules that regulate biochemical reactions (e.g., Vitamin C, Vitamin D).
  • Minerals: Inorganic elements essential for structural roles (calcium in bones) and nerve transmission (sodium, potassium).

Lipids are organic molecules composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen that are insoluble in water (hydrophobic). The main types are triglycerides (fats), phospholipids, and sterols (cholesterol).

Primary Functions:

  • Energy Storage: Highly concentrated energy store (9 kcal/g vs 4 kcal/g in carbs/proteins).
  • Structural Support: Phospholipids build the lipid bilayer of all cellular membranes.
  • Insulation & Protection: Adipose tissue insulates organs against heat loss and mechanical shock.
  • Hormone Synthesis: Cholesterol serves as the precursor for steroid hormones (estrogen, testosterone, cortisol).

Carbohydrates are the primary fuel source for ATP synthesis through cellular respiration. The brain and red blood cells rely exclusively on glucose under normal physiological conditions.

  • Simple Carbohydrates: Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) and disaccharides (sucrose, lactose) provide immediate energy.
  • Complex Carbohydrates: Polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose/fiber). Starch and glycogen store energy in plants and animals respectively, while fiber helps in bowel regulation.
  • Macronutrients: Nutrients required in large amounts (grams) daily. They supply chemical energy and structural blocks. Examples: Carbohydrates, Lipids, Proteins, and Water.
  • Micronutrients: Nutrients required in trace amounts (micrograms or milligrams) daily. They do not yield energy but are vital for metabolism and biochemical signaling. Examples: Vitamins (Vitamin C, B-complex) and Minerals (Iron, Calcium, Zinc).

Proteins are polymers composed of amino acid monomers linked by peptide bonds. Unlike carbs and lipids, they contain Nitrogen (N) along with C, H, and O.

Physiological Importance:

  • Enzymatic Activity: All biological enzymes are proteins that speed up reactions millions of times.
  • Cellular Structure: Form structural tissues like muscle, collagen in joints, hair (keratin), and skin.
  • Defense: Antibodies that combat pathogens are specialized proteins.
  • Transport: Hemoglobin is a protein that transports oxygen in the bloodstream.

Water is essential because the body cannot synthesize it in sufficient quantities to sustain life, and it constitutes over 60% of total body weight.

Key Functions:

  • Reaction Medium: Acts as the solvent for all biochemical reactions in the cytosol.
  • Transport Medium: Forms plasma in blood to transport nutrients, oxygen, and metabolic waste.
  • Thermoregulation: Absorb and dissipate heat via sweating, keeping core body temperature stable.
  • Lubrication & Cushioning: Surrounds joints (synovial fluid) and cushions vital organs.
  • Major Minerals: Required in amounts > 100 mg/day. Examples: Calcium (Ca), Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Phosphorus (P), Magnesium (Mg), Chlorine (Cl), and Sulfur (S).
  • Trace Minerals: Required in amounts < 100 mg/day. Examples: Iron (Fe), Zinc (Zn), Iodine (I), Fluoride (F), Copper (Cu), Selenium (Se).
  • Calcium Role: Critical for bone and tooth structure, skeletal muscle contraction, nerve impulse propagation, and blood clotting cascades.
  • Iron Role: Central component of the heme group in hemoglobin and myoglobin, allowing oxygen transport in blood and muscle.

The human digestive system breaks down food mechanically and chemically to absorb nutrients and eliminate waste. The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is a continuous muscular tube consisting of:

  1. Mouth: Ingestion, mechanical mastication, and starch digestion.
  2. Pharynx: Swallowing passage.
  3. Oesophagus: Moves bolus down using peristalsis.
  4. Stomach: Acid digestion and churning of protein.
  5. Small Intestine: Primary absorption of nutrients.
  6. Large Intestine: Absorbs water and minerals.
  7. Rectum & Anus: Storage and expulsion of waste.
  • Mechanical Digestion: Chewing (mastication) by teeth grinds food into smaller pieces, increasing its surface area for enzymes.
  • Chemical Digestion: Salivary glands release saliva, which contains:
    • Salivary Amylase: Breaks complex starch molecules into maltose (simple disaccharide).
    • Lysozyme: Weak antibacterial agent protecting the mouth.
    • Mucus: Lubricates the food particles into a cohesive ball (bolus) for easy swallowing.

The stomach acts as a mixing chamber and acid digester. Its gastric juices contain three critical components:

  • HCl (Hydrochloric Acid): Lowers gastric pH to ~1.5-2.0. It kills ingested bacteria, denatures dietary proteins, and activates inactive pepsinogen into active pepsin.
  • Pepsin: An active protease that breaks down long protein chains into smaller peptides.
  • Mucus: Forms a thick, alkaline protective lining over the stomach walls, preventing the HCl and pepsin from digesting the stomach's own tissue (preventing ulcers).

The small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) is where almost all nutrient absorption occurs.

  • Digestion: Receives bile from the liver (to emulsify fats) and pancreatic enzymes (amylase, lipase, trypsin) to complete chemical breakdown of macromolecules.
  • Surface Area Adaptation: The inner lining is highly folded into villi and cellular microvilli (called the brush border), increasing the effective surface area by ~600 times for passive and active absorption.
  • Capillary Network: Nutrients enter blood capillaries, while lipids enter lymphatic vessels called lacteals.
  • Large Intestine (Colon): Absorbs water and remaining electrolytes from undigested material. It houses beneficial gut bacteria that produce Vitamin K.
  • Rectum: Temporary storage compartment that holds feces and triggers sensory nerves signaling the urge to defecate.
  • Anus: Consists of internal (involuntary) and external (voluntary) sphincters that regulate the controlled elimination of waste.

Accessory organs assist in chemical digestion without food passing through them:

  • Liver: Synthesizes bile salts which emulsify large fat globules into tiny droplets, allowing lipase enzymes to work.
  • Gallbladder: Stores, concentrates, and releases bile into the duodenum via the common bile duct in response to fatty meals.
  • Pancreas: Synthesizes and secretes pancreatic juice containing bicarbonate (neutralizes stomach acid) and digestive enzymes: amylase (carbs), lipase (fats), and trypsin (proteins).
  • Breathing (Pulmonary Ventilation): The mechanical, physical process of inhaling oxygen and exhaling carbon dioxide by expanding and contracting the thoracic cavity.
  • Cellular Respiration: The metabolic, biochemical process occurring inside cells (cytoplasm and mitochondria) where glucose is broken down to generate ATP energy.

Anatomy: Air enters Nasal Cavity → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli.

Gas Exchange Mechanism: Occurs at the alveoli. Due to partial pressure differences, Oxygen diffuses across the thin alveolar wall and capillary endothelium into deoxygenated blood where it binds to hemoglobin. Concurrently, Carbon Dioxide diffuses from blood into alveoli to be exhaled.

This process relies on passive diffusion across the **alveolar-capillary membrane** (thickness < 0.5 micrometers).

  • In inhaled air within alveoli, partial pressure of oxygen ($P_{O2}$) is high (~104 mmHg), whereas in deoxygenated blood in capillaries it is low (~40 mmHg). Oxygen diffuses from alveoli into blood.
  • Conversely, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide ($P_{CO2}$) in blood is high (~45 mmHg) compared to alveolar air (~40 mmHg). CO₂ diffuses into alveoli.

The **excretory system** removes metabolic waste, maintains water and electrolyte balances, and regulates blood pressure and pH.

Role of Kidneys: Act as the primary blood filtration organs. They filter ~180 liters of blood daily, extracting waste molecules like urea and excess ions while reabsorbing essential water, glucose, and sodium to maintain homeostasis.

A nephron consists of the renal corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) and renal tubules (Proximal Tubule, Loop of Henle, Distal Tubule, Collecting Duct).

Three Processes of Urine Formation:

  • Ultrafiltration: High blood pressure forces water and solutes out of the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule, forming filtrate.
  • Tubular Reabsorption: Useful substances (glucose, amino acids, 99% of water) are actively or passively reabsorbed from filtrate back into blood capillaries.
  • Tubular Secretion: Wastes like hydrogen ions, potassium, and drugs are actively transported from blood into tubules to be excreted in urine.

An **artificial organ** is a man-made device integrated into the human body to replace or support the function of a diseased or damaged natural organ.

Engineering Design Challenges:

  • Biocompatibility: Materials must not trigger immune reactions or chronic inflammation.
  • Hemocompatibility: Devices in contact with blood must avoid causing shear stress, blood cell lysis (hemolysis), or clot formation (thrombosis).
  • Power Supply: Implantable systems need long-lasting batteries or wireless inductive power transmission.
  • Control Systems: Devices must adapt dynamically to physiological changes (e.g., matching pacemaker speed to physical activity).

An **artificial cardiac pacemaker** replaces a faulty sinoatrial (SA) node.

  • Working Principle: Contains a lithium battery, sensing circuits, and timing circuits. It continuously monitors the heart's electrical rhythm via electrodes inserted in the right atrium or ventricle.
  • Action: If the heart's natural rate drops below a preset limit (e.g., 60 bpm), the pulse generator fires electrical pulses (~1-5 volts) that depolarize cardiac muscle fibers, triggering contraction and maintaining healthy cardiac output.

An **artificial heart** (or Total Artificial Heart, TAH) replaces both ventricles to restore systemic and pulmonary blood flow during end-stage heart failure.

  • Design: Features polymer chambers, mechanical valves (to ensure one-way flow), and diaphragm-like pistons.
  • Actuation: Driven pneumatically or electromagnetically by an external controller connected via a transcutaneous driveline. The piston compresses the chambers alternately to mimic heartbeats.

An **artificial lung** (oxygenator) performs gas exchange outside the body during open-heart surgery:

  • Design: Contains thousands of microporous hollow polypropylene fibers.
  • Mechanism: Blood passes around the outside of the fibers, while pure oxygen (sweep gas) flows inside the fibers.
  • Diffusion: Oxygen diffuses through the micropores into the blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into the fibers to be vented, replicating alveolar gas exchange.

Hemodialysis cleanses blood of metabolic toxins (urea, creatinine) via mass transfer:

  • Semi-permeable Membrane: Blood flows inside cellulose/synthetic hollow fibers, while a cleaning fluid (dialysate) flows outside.
  • Countercurrent Flow: Blood and dialysate flow in opposite directions, maintaining a high concentration gradient along the entire dialyzer length.
  • Diffusion & Ultrafiltration: Waste diffuses down its gradient from blood into dialysate. Excess water is pushed out by applying hydrostatic pressure (ultrafiltration).
